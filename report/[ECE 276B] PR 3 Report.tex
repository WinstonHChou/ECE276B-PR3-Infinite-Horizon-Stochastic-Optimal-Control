\begin{abstract}
This report presents the implementation and evaluation of two optimal control strategies for trajectory tracking of a unicycle car model in a stochastic environment with obstacles. We first implement a receding-horizon Certainty Equivalent Control (CEC) scheme that solves a Non-Linear Programming (NLP) problem at each time step. Second, we utilize Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI) to solve the infinite-horizon stochastic optimal control problem through value iteration on a discretized state space. We compare the performance of both methods in tracking a complex lemniscate trajectory while maintaining safety. Our results highlight the trade-offs between online optimization (CEC) and offline value function pre-computation (GPI) regarding tracking accuracy and real-time feasibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Autonomous robot navigation requires precise trajectory tracking capabilities alongside robust obstacle avoidance. In many practical applications, the robot's dynamics are non-linear and subject to stochastic disturbances, while the environment contains complex constraints. This project focuses on the trajectory tracking problem for a unicycle robot tasked with following a periodic lemniscate path in the presence of circular obstacles and motion noise.

We explore two foundational approaches in optimal control:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Certainty Equivalent Control (CEC):} A receding-horizon approach that simplifies the stochastic control problem by solving a deterministic optimization problem at each step. By ignoring noise and assuming the most likely transition, CEC leverages efficient NLP solvers to find locally optimal control sequences.
    \item \textbf{Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI):} A dynamic programming-based approach that addresses the stochasticity directly. By discretizing the error-state space, GPI computes a value function and an optimal policy offline that minimizes the expected infinite-horizon discounted cost.
\end{enumerate}

This report details the mathematical formulation of the unicycle dynamics and the tracking objective, describes the implementation of both algorithms, and provides a comparative analysis of their tracking performance and computational characteristics.

\section{Problem Statement}

The robot is modeled as a unicycle car operating in a 2D environment $\mathcal{W} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ with state $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and control $u_t \in \mathcal{U} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$.

\subsection{Robot Dynamics}
The continuous-time kinematics are given by:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{p}_x \\ \dot{p}_y \\ \dot{\theta} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v \cos \theta \\ v \sin \theta \\ \omega \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $v$ and $\omega$ are the linear and angular velocities. The discrete-time stochastic dynamics used in this project assume exact integration over a time step $\Delta t$:
\begin{equation}
    x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k) + w_k, \quad w_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)
\end{equation}
where $w_k$ represents the process noise.

\subsection{Objective Function}
The goal is to follow a reference trajectory $x_{ref}(t)$. We define the tracking error as $e_k = x_k - x_{ref}(k \Delta t)$. The objective is to minimize the expected infinite-horizon discounted cost:
\begin{equation}
    J(x_0) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k \ell(e_k, u_k) \right]
\end{equation}
where $\gamma \in (0, 1)$ is the discount factor and $\ell(e, u)$ is the stage cost:
\begin{equation}
    \ell(e, u) = e_{pos}^T Q e_{pos} + q(1 - \cos e_{\theta})^2 + u^T R u
\end{equation}
Here $e_{pos} = [e_x, e_y]^T$, while $Q$, $R$, and $q$ are weighting parameters.

\subsection{Constraints}
The control inputs are constrained within $[v_{min}, v_{max}]$ and $[\omega_{min}, \omega_{max}]$. Additionally, the robot must avoid a set of $n$ circular obstacles $\mathcal{O} = \{O_j\}_{j=1}^n$ and remain within the world boundaries. Safety is enforced by ensuring the distance to any obstacle center $p_{O,j}$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    \| p_x - p_{O,j} \|_2 \geq r_j + r_{robot}
\end{equation}
where $r_j$ is the obstacle radius and $r_{robot}$ is the robot's collision margin.

\section{Technical Approach}

The trajectory tracking problem is addressed using two distinct paradigms: receding-horizon optimization and infinite-horizon value iteration.

\subsection{Certainty Equivalent Control (CEC)}
CEC simplifies the stochastic optimal control problem by assuming that process noise is zero during the planning horizon. At each time step $k$, we solve a finite-horizon deterministic optimal control problem:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{u_{k:k+T-1}} \sum_{i=k}^{k+T-1} \gamma^{i-k} \ell(e_i, u_i) + \gamma^T \ell_{term}(e_{k+T})
\end{equation}
subject to the unicycle dynamics and control/state constraints. We implement this using the CasADi framework with the $ipopt$ solver. Only the first control $u_k^*$ is applied, and the optimization is repeated at the next time step.

\subsection{Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI)}
GPI addresses the infinite-horizon problem by pre-computing a stationary optimal policy $\pi^*(e)$. The continuous error-state space $\mathcal{E}$ is discretized into a finite grid $\mathcal{S}_{grid}$. We perform two interleaved steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Policy Evaluation:} Estimate the value function $V^\pi$ for the current policy $\pi$.
    \item \textbf{Policy Improvement:} Update the policy by selecting the action that minimizes the one-step lookahead cost:
    \begin{equation}
        \pi'(e) = \arg\min_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \left\{ \ell(e, u) + \gamma \sum_{e'} P(e'|e, u) V^\pi(e') \right\}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
To scale to large grids, we utilize Numba-accelerated JIT compilation for the transition matrix and cost computations, and enforce periodic wrap-around ($V_T = V_0$) for the lemniscate trajectory.

\section{Results}

\subsection{CEC Performance}
The CEC controller demonstrates robust tracking of the lemniscate trajectory in deterministic settings. However, as process noise increases, the receding-horizon solution may drift if the horizon $T$ is too short.

\subsection{GPI Performance}
The GPI policy provides a fast lookup-based control law. Its performance is heavily dependent on the discretization resolution of the error states. We analyze the tracking error convergence and the impact of the discount factor $\gamma$.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}
We compare CEC and GPI across several metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Computational Cost:} Online optimization time (CEC) vs. offline pre-computation time and online lookup time (GPI).
    \item \textbf{Tracking Error:} Cumulative translation and rotation error.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

In this project, we implemented and compared CEC and GPI for trajectory tracking of a unicycle robot. CEC offers flexibility in handling obstacle constraints through online optimization, while GPI provides a computationally efficient feedback law once the value function has converged.
